How your team framed it, round by round

Your framing“This is a technical monitoring matter — Engineering owns it, no broader leadership framing needed.”

Chosen in the first 17 minutes, on the least evidence you would ever have. It located the problem inside one function and set the vocabulary every later round inherited.

02When Ambiguity Becomes Discussable
Your framing“Contained operational matter — hold the language. Nothing has technically changed since Round 1.”

What the five constructs mean

Early Signal Legitimization

Higher is stronger

Whether a weak, deniable signal was given standing — named, owned and made discussable — before anything forced the issue.

Silence Accumulation

Higher is more concerning

Concern that was felt but not voiced. Silence is rarely a single choice; it compounds, and each unspoken round makes the next one easier.

Framing Commitment

Higher is more concerning

How tightly the organisation held its first description of the problem once that description became public inside the company.

Authority Centralization

Higher is more concerning

How far judgment collapsed upward — functions deferring to the CEO rather than exercising the authority their role already carried.

Option Space Contraction

Higher is more concerning

How much room to act the team still had at the end. Early framing plus accumulated silence quietly removes choices long before anyone notices they're gone.

Where your trajectory was set

Round 1, at roughly the seven-minute mark. Engineering tagged the anomaly as a known issue to monitor and Operations logged it as under observation — two reasonable calls that, together, produced an organisational position nobody had actually decided to take.

From there the pattern is consistent rather than dramatic. Your CHRO issued escalation guidance in Round 1 and kept raising the culture signal in Rounds 3 and 4; that voice existed and did not carry. The CFO repeated the same investor reassurance twice, which is what prompted the investor to ask why the answer never changed. By Round 3 the move to formalise reporting was real — but it arrived after the regulator asked, which is a different thing from arriving because the team decided.

Nothing here required anyone to behave badly. The anomaly never worsened, no customer complained, and every individual decision was defensible on its own. That is the finding worth taking away.

Questions to sit with
  • Round 1Which specific cue in the CRO's note would you now treat as decision-relevant rather than noise — and what would it have cost you to say so at minute seven?
  • Round 2Did your team converge on “contained” because the evidence supported it, or because disagreement felt expensive? Who spoke first, and did that decide it?
  • Round 3Whose interests were you protecting on the board agenda — the board's, the regulator's, or your own credibility?
  • Round 4Was the whistle-channel inquiry treated as a data point or as a threat? What does that say about the culture the four rounds actually built?
  • Across the simulationTwo of your decisions expired unanswered. In a real quarter, what would those two silences have looked like to the people waiting on them?
